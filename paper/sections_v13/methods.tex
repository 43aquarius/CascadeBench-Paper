\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Design overview}

CascadeBench measures how acoustic channel degradations propagate through
commercial speech recognition and translation services that can only be
accessed through their public interfaces. The design is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}. A bilingual corpus of 480 sentences is synthesised to
audio with multiple neural voices, degraded by a grid of controlled channel
impairments, and submitted to five recognition systems and four speech
translation paths. Every stage of the pipeline is scripted with fixed random
seeds, and every model response together with its round-trip latency is
logged at the sentence level, so that the complete measurement can be
re-executed as providers update their services.

\subsection{Corpus}

The corpus contains 240 Mandarin Chinese and 240 English sentences, balanced
across four content domains and three length strata, twenty sentences per
cell. The domains are broadcast news, everyday conversation, technical and
scientific exposition, and a numerals-and-entities stratum rich in dates,
quantities, addresses, and named entities, which is known to stress
recognition and inverse text normalisation. Short sentences contain 8 to 14
Chinese characters or 5 to 9 English words, medium sentences 15 to 25
characters or 10 to 17 words, and long sentences 26 to 39 characters or 18 to
24 words. The mean length is 19.3 characters for Chinese and 12.0 words for
English. Sentences were authored to be self-contained and
factually neutral, to avoid brand names and transliterated loanwords whose
recognition would depend on provider-specific vocabulary rather than on
acoustic processing, and to distribute numerical expressions across date,
time, quantity, price, and identifier formats within the numerals domain. All numbers are written out in words in the corpus text, for example
two thousand twenty five, so that the spoken realisation is unambiguous and
the reference transcript matches the synthesis input exactly.

Each sentence was synthesised by a neural speech synthesis service (Sambert,
Alibaba Cloud Model Studio) using two distinct voices per language, a male
and a female voice for Chinese and two native-sounding voices for English,
with sentences alternating between the voices so that each voice realises 120
sentences per language. Synthesis produced 16~kHz single-channel PCM audio.
The 40 babble noise source tracks described below were synthesised with
six additional voices that are disjoint from the four test voices. Using
synthesis rather than recorded speech has two methodological advantages. The
reference transcript is exact by construction, which removes the transcription
noise that limits error floor measurement in recorded corpora, and the
acoustic properties of the test material can be regenerated at any time,
which decouples the benchmark from the licensing and availability constraints
of recorded corpora such as AISHELL-1~\cite{bu2017aishell} or
LibriSpeech-derived material~\cite{zen2019libritts}. The corresponding
limitation, that synthetic speech does not sample the full variability of
spontaneous human speech, is examined in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Acoustic degradation grid}

Terminology is fixed once here and used throughout. A \emph{degradation
family} is one acoustic impairment mechanism; seven families are studied:
babble noise, reverberation, narrowbanding, codec compression, packet loss,
clipping, and tempo distortion. A \emph{degradation condition} is a family
at a specified severity, for example babble at $-5$~dB; the main grid
contributes eight conditions (babble at 10 and 0~dB plus six non-noise
conditions) and the severity sweep contributes six further babble levels,
fourteen degradation conditions in all. An \emph{operating point} is a
cascade evaluation condition, the fourteen degradation conditions plus the
clean reference, fifteen in total; the third cascade covers twelve of them.

Eight channel conditions plus the clean control form the main grid. The
conditions were selected to span the canonical impairment families that
speech services encounter between a talker and a data centre.

Additive babble noise is the most extensively sampled family. Babble was
constructed by concatenating randomly selected segments from the 40 disjoint
noise-source tracks described above, mixing three such streams, and
normalising the mixture, which produces speech-shaped noise with realistic
spectral and temporal structure rather than stationary synthetic noise. Speech-shaped interference was chosen over stationary
noise because it is the harder and the more ecologically valid case: babble
exhibits the temporal modulation and spectral occupancy of competing talkers,
masking the target in the same time-frequency regions that carry linguistic
information, whereas stationary noise is partly countered by any
spectral-subtraction-style front-end that a provider may have deployed
internally. The
noise was scaled against the active-speech level of each utterance, measured
as the mean energy of frames above the 60th energy percentile, to hit target
signal-to-noise ratios of 20, 15, 10, 5, 2.5, 0, $-5$, and $-10$~dB.
Calibration was verified
by re-measuring the realised signal-to-noise ratio of the mixed signals
against the clean signal; the realised values matched the active-level targets
within 0.1~dB on average over the 20 to 0~dB ladder and within 0.6~dB at the
$-10$~dB point. The severity convention deserves emphasis because it is not
the only possible one: because the 60th-percentile active-level rule ignores
inter-speech pauses, the total-power signal-to-noise ratio of the same
signals runs 1.8 to 2.2~dB below the stated active-level values, so a
reader comparing against total-power conventions should shift our levels
by approximately $-2$~dB. The 10~dB and 0~dB points enter the main grid, and
the full eight-point ladder forms the severity sweep on a stratified
144-sentence subset, 72 sentences per language, sampled evenly from every
domain-by-length cell. For the telephone-band recogniser, the degraded
signals are generated at 16~kHz first and then downsampled to 8~kHz, so the
specialist sees the same noise realisation band-limited to its native input
bandwidth rather than noise generated inside its band.

Reverberation was applied by convolving each signal with a synthetic room
impulse response of the exponentially decaying noise
form~\cite{allen1979image,habets2006room} with a direct-path impulse and a
reverberation time of 0.5~s. We measured the acoustic properties of the
realised impulse responses: Schroeder $T_{60}$ is 0.51~s as designed, the
clarity ratio $C_{50}$ is $+4.9$~dB, and the direct-to-reverberant ratio is
$-27.6$~dB, the last reflecting the exponentially decaying diffuse tail of
this synthesis, which concentrates reverberant energy in a spectrally flat
late field rather than in discrete early reflections. The condition is
therefore best read as diffuse late reverberation at a moderate clarity
ratio rather than as an emulation of a specific small room, and its contrast
with real recorded impulse responses, whose early-reflection structure
colours the spectrum more strongly, is examined in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Narrowband degradation resamples each
signal to 8~kHz and back to 16~kHz with polyphase anti-aliasing filters,
which removes the 4 to 8~kHz band exactly as a telephone channel does; the
telephone-specialised recogniser receives the 8~kHz signal natively. Codec
degradation encodes each signal with the Opus codec~\cite{valin2012definition}
at 8~kbit/s mono, in the default voip application mode of the libopus
encoder as shipped by ffmpeg 7.1.5, and decodes it back. Measurement of the
decoded signals shows that at this bitrate the encoder operates effectively
as a narrowband coder: the decoded output retains less than 0.1 percent of
clean high-band energy above 4~kHz, so the codec and narrowband conditions
are not fully independent stressors of high-frequency content. Packet loss
zeroes 15 percent of 20~ms frames chosen uniformly at random, emulating
lossy transport without jitter compensation. Clipping applies hard amplitude
limiting at 0.3 of full scale, which with a mean input peak of 0.6 clips
about 0.6 percent of samples. Tempo distortion resamples the time axis by a
factor of 1.15 with pitch preservation using the ffmpeg atempo filter; the
spectral centroid is preserved within one percent, no level renormalisation
is applied after any degradation, and the peak-normalised output convention
follows the synthesis. All random draws use per-sentence and per-condition
seeds derived deterministically from a CRC32 digest of the sentence
identifier and condition name, and the released audio tree, generated with
the same rule, is shipped alongside the logs so that the original
measurements are exactly reproducible while fresh regenerations remain
deterministic.

Formally, for a clean signal $x[n]$ of length $N$ and a babble realisation
$b[n]$, the mixed signal at level $\rho$~dB is
\begin{equation}
y[n] = x[n] + \sigma\, b[n], \qquad
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{P_{\mathrm{act}}(x)}{10^{\rho/10}\, \overline{P}(b)}},
\label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{P}(b)$ is the mean power of the babble and
$P_{\mathrm{act}}(x)$ is the active-speech power estimated from frames whose
energy exceeds the 60th energy percentile of the utterance, which
approximates the ITU-T P.56 active-level convention with a fixed percentile
rule. The reverberation impulse response is
\begin{equation}
h[n] = \delta[n] + v[n]\, e^{-6.9\, n / (f_s T_{60})}, \qquad n = 0, \dots,
\lceil f_s T_{60} \rceil,
\label{eq:rir}
\end{equation}
with $v[n]$ unit-variance white noise, $f_s = 16$~kHz, $T_{60} = 0.5$~s, and
$h$ normalised to unit energy, and the reverberant signal is the truncated
convolution of $x$ with $h$ rescaled to the peak amplitude of $x$. Packet
loss zeroes each independent 20~ms frame with probability $p = 0.15$,
\begin{equation}
y[n] = m_k\, x[n], \quad n \in \text{frame } k, \qquad m_k \sim
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1 - p),
\label{eq:pl}
\end{equation}
and clipping applies $y[n] = \operatorname{sign}(x[n]) \min(|x[n]|, 0.3)$.
Narrowbanding resamples by factors of one half and two with polyphase
anti-aliasing filters, codec degradation round-trips the signal through the
Opus encoder at 8~kbit/s, and tempo distortion applies a pitch-preserving
time-scale modification of factor 1.15.

\subsection{Real-speech validation protocol}
\label{sec:methods:real}

Because the main corpus is synthesised, we replicated the core of the
design on human-recorded speech with human-authored ground-truth
transcripts, real measured impulse responses, and real noise, addressing
the realism concerns that a purely synthetic pipeline invites. The
validation subset contains 150 utterances: 75 Mandarin sentences drawn
from the AISHELL-1 test set~\cite{bu2017aishell} (11 speakers) and 75
English sentences from the LibriSpeech test-clean
set~\cite{panayotov2015librispeech} (16 speakers), filtered to 2.5--12~s
and level-normalised to the active-speech RMS of the synthetic corpus so
that signal-to-noise ratios remain comparable across pipelines. Unlike the
synthetic corpus, the reference transcripts are human transcriptions that
never passed through any vendor's synthesiser, which makes the recognition
references fully provider-independent.

Three degradation families are applied to each utterance, giving nine
conditions in total (clean, real reverberation, six babble SNRs, transport
noise), all constructed to
be directly comparable to synthetic counterparts. First, real-room
reverberation convolves each utterance with one of three measured impulse
responses from the MIT Impulse Response Survey~\cite{traer2016statistics},
selected because their Schroeder-integrated reverberation times (0.495,
0.499, and 0.500~s) match the synthetic $T_{60} = 0.5$~s condition almost
exactly while spanning a realistic range of direct-to-reverberant ratios
($-0.9$, $+1.3$, and $+13.1$~dB); the three rooms are rotated across
utterances, and the same three responses, with the same rotation, are
additionally convolved with the full 480-sentence synthetic corpus as a
bridge condition that isolates impulse-response realism from speech
corpus realism. Second, real multi-talker babble is constructed with exactly
the same three-stream mixing algorithm as the synthetic babble, but from
40 real recorded utterances (20 AISHELL-1 training speakers and 20
LibriSpeech dev-other speakers, all disjoint from the test speakers),
mixed with the target speech at six SNR levels of $+10$, $+5$, $+2.5$, $0$,
$-2.5$, and $-5$~dB, so that the crossing levels of the degradation knee are
interpolated between adjacent measured points rather than extrapolated from
a three-level tail. Third, real non-speech transport noise, drawn from the
train category of the ESC-50 environmental sound corpus~\cite{piczak2015esc},
is mixed at 0~dB SNR to probe whether the noise-type conclusions transfer
beyond speech-shaped interference. Acoustic verification of all degraded
signals follows the main protocol: median STOI is 0.77 for real reverberation
and 0.71, 0.62, 0.50 for real babble at $+5$, $0$, $-5$~dB.

Four recognition systems (both audio-LLM systems and both streaming
specialists) and the Qwen-Audio--cascade and LiveTranslate end-to-end
paths are evaluated on all real-speech conditions (clean, real reverb, the
six real-babble SNRs, transport noise); the omni system is excluded because
its complimentary quota was exhausted. Two external components extend the
validation beyond the single vendor of the main grid. The open-weights
Whisper small model~\cite{radford2023robust} is executed locally with the
faster-whisper int8 CPU runtime and its default decoding configuration,
with an explicit Mandarin initial prompt, on the clean
and three hardest babble conditions as a non-vendor recognition baseline;
it makes no API calls and its per-call latencies (median 3.9~s, up to 34~s on
the hardest conditions) confirm local execution. The GLM large language
model of a second vendor (Z.ai), accessed as a hosted
API~\cite{glm2026api,glm2025tech}, is used in two roles: as a third
translation
reference, generated from the clean transcripts of both the synthetic and
the real corpora, and as an alternative cascade backend, re-translating the
Qwen-Audio recognition outputs of the four core real-speech conditions
(clean, $+5$, $0$, $-5$~dB real babble) so that the pass-through relation
can be re-fitted on a translation engine disjoint from the vendor of every
recognition frontend. Translation references for the validation subset are
produced by the oracle protocol: the cascade translation engine applied to
the human ground-truth transcripts, so the recognition stage is the only
difference between the cascade and its own upper bound. This construction
is deliberate for measuring pass-through within the cascade, but it also
means that this reference and the cascade share one translation engine:
absolute BLEU comparisons against it favour the cascade over any
end-to-end path by construction, so the end-to-end comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:results:real} is drawn in terms of within-path relative
losses, which are stable across reference choices, and absolute gaps are
interpreted only jointly with the three-reference analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:results:dual}. All validation API calls follow the main
logging, retry, and scoring infrastructure, and the complete preparation
pipeline (dataset selection, impulse-response measurement, babble
construction) is seeded and released alongside the main corpus.

\subsection{Systems under test}

Table~\ref{tab:systems} lists the systems. The recognition systems of the
main grid span
three product classes as served by a single commercial provider
(Alibaba Cloud Model Studio, Beijing region) during October 2026, accessed
through their documented interfaces; the validation components add one
open-weights model executed locally and one commercial model of a second
vendor, so that vendor and product class are separated wherever the design
allows. The specialised family consists of
streaming recognisers in the Paraformer lineage~\cite{gao2023funasr}, a
16~kHz model and an 8~kHz telephone-band model. The LLM-ASR family consists
of Qwen-Audio 3.1 Flash~\cite{chu2024qwen2audio,qwenaudio31card} and Qwen3-ASR Flash, audio
input models built on large language model backbones. The multimodal family
consists of Qwen3.8-Omni Flash, a general-purpose omni-modal foundation
model~\cite{qwen2.5omni2025,qwenomni38card} queried with a transcription instruction. The
external components are Whisper small~\cite{radford2023robust}, the
open-weights recognition model, run locally with the faster-whisper int8
CPU runtime, and the GLM large language model of a second vendor
(Z.ai)~\cite{glm2026api}, from the GLM foundation-model
family~\cite{glm2025tech}, used as a reference generator and as an
alternative cascade backend. All
systems were accessed in their default configurations, since a consumer of a
black-box service cannot apply front-end training or adaptation, and the
question of interest is precisely how the services behave out of the box.
The streaming recognisers were driven by streaming local audio over their
realtime WebSocket interface, sending each utterance as 16~kHz or 8~kHz PCM
frames at the maximum accepted rate and collecting sentence-final segments.
The LLM-ASR and omni systems received base64-encoded whole utterances in
their audio-input message format, the omni model together with an
instruction prompt for transcription or translation. The streaming
translation service was driven over its realtime interface with a session
configuration specifying text-only output and the target language, sending
raw PCM frames and collecting both the source transcript and the translated
text that the service emits, and the text translation engine received
source text with explicit source and target language tags. Every call was
executed with an automatic retry policy of up to six attempts with
exponential backoff on throttling, timeout, and connection failures, and no
other tuning parameters were exposed or set. The speech translation systems are the text translation
engine qwen-mt-uni~\cite{qwenmtcard}, used as the second stage of three cascades built on the
16~kHz specialised recogniser, on the Qwen-Audio 3.1 recogniser, and on the
Qwen3-ASR recogniser, and two
end-to-end paths: the omni foundation model with an audio translation
instruction, and a dedicated streaming speech translation service
(LiveTranslate) that internally combines a streaming frontend recogniser
with a translation model. The second-backend validation re-runs the cascade
translation stage with the GLM model of the second vendor in place of
qwen-mt-uni on the same
recognition outputs. The internal frontend of the streaming translation
service, which its responses expose as source transcripts, is additionally
scored as a seventh recognition system on the severity sweep; the five
purchasable recognition systems of Table~\ref{tab:systems} are therefore
complemented by this embedded frontend and by Whisper wherever they are
available. All API calls were executed on
free-tier quotas with the stop-on-exhaustion option enabled, which both
bounds the study at zero monetary cost and documents the practical quota
requirements of such a measurement. All systems were accessed with their
default decoding configurations and no exposed sampling parameters were
overridden, so the repetition analysis of Section~\ref{sec:results:latency}
characterises the services as a consumer actually receives them.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

Recognition accuracy is scored as the character error rate (CER) for Chinese
and the word error rate (WER) for English, computed with the standard
Levenshtein alignment~\cite{levenshtein1966binary} after a normalisation
pipeline that is applied identically to references and hypotheses. The
normalisation performs Unicode compatibility folding, case folding,
punctuation removal, and, critically, canonicalisation of numeric
expressions. Because commercial recognisers differ in their inverse text
normalisation conventions, with the same spoken quantity rendered as twenty
six, 26, or two six depending on the system, both sides of every comparison
are mapped to a canonical digit form by rules that handle Chinese and
English cardinal numbers, digit-by-digit readings, time expressions, decimal
fractions, and scale multipliers such as wan and yi on the Chinese side and
million and billion on the English side, with ordinal expressions masked to
a common token. The rule set was developed and verified on the clean
condition, where residual normalisation artefacts were inspected sentence by
sentence until the clean-condition error rates reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results} reflect genuine recognition behaviour rather than
scoring artefacts. Utterances for which a service returns no transcript at
all, a behaviour documented in Section~\ref{sec:results}, are scored as
complete failures with an error rate of one.

Translation quality is scored with corpus-level BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu}
using sacreBLEU~\cite{post2018call} with the 13a tokeniser for English
targets and the character-level tokeniser for Chinese targets, and with
chrF~\cite{popovic2015chrf} as a second string-matching metric; sacreBLEU
signatures are fixed in the released scripts (BLEU: effective order 1,
case-insensitive, 13a for English and zh character tokenisation for
Chinese). Because the
corpus is direction-symmetric, BLEU is computed separately for the
Chinese-to-English and English-to-Chinese halves and macro-averaged, and
the number of scored sentences $n$ is stated for every cell because
coverage varies where frontends refuse or sessions drop. Two scoring
protocols are distinguished wherever they differ: quality conditional on a
non-empty translation, and effective quality in which a refused frontend
output is scored as an empty translation. Corpus-level BLEU reacts to
empties only through the brevity penalty, which saturates when hypotheses
are longer than references, so the refusal-sensitive comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:results:cascade} is anchored on chrF as well. Every
hypothesis is scored against three reference sets with deliberately
distinct family relationships. The primary reference is
produced by Qwen3.8-Max~\cite{qwen3technical2025}, the strongest
general-purpose text LLM of the same provider, translating the clean
reference transcripts; it shares a vendor, though not a product line, with
the translation systems under test. The secondary reference is the oracle
cascade output, that is, the cascade translation engine applied to the clean
reference transcripts, which is same-engine with every cascade. The
tertiary reference is generated by the GLM model of a second
vendor~\cite{glm2026api,glm2025tech},
translating the same clean transcripts,
and is therefore family-disjoint from every system under test; the family
relationships of all systems and references are disclosed in
Table~\ref{tab:systems} and revisited in
Section~\ref{sec:results:dual}. The three-reference design exposes a bias
that Section~\ref{sec:results:dual} quantifies: automatic scoring against
machine-generated references systematically favours systems from the same
model family as the reference generator, so conclusions about absolute
quality depend on the choice of reference, whereas conclusions about
degradation are stable across references. The text normalisation rules were developed by
inspecting clean-condition outputs until residual mismatches ceased; a
sensitivity analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:normalisation} re-scores the
main grid without numeral canonicalisation and shows that the
system ordering is preserved while clean-condition gaps compress. To
quantify run-to-run variability of the non-deterministic LLM services, the
Qwen-Audio and Qwen3-ASR systems were re-run twice on the 0~dB babble and
tempo conditions; condition-level error rates vary by at most 0.9
percentage points across the three runs, although one in five individual
transcripts differs between runs (Section~\ref{sec:results:latency}).

Latency is measured as the wall-clock round trip of each API call from
dispatch to the complete response, recorded per call. The text translation
stage was additionally benchmarked with 120 timed calls on
oracle sentences, because the main cascade logs do not carry per-call
timing; cascade latency is therefore reported as the sum of the recognition
and translation stage means, which is the quantity a consumer experiences
when the two stages are invoked sequentially. Streaming systems are fed
audio at the maximum rate their interface accepts, so the measurement
reflects service-side processing rather than client-side throttling, and it
measures whole-utterance turnaround rather than first-partial latency;
streaming systems would show substantially lower first-partial latencies
than the whole-utterance figures reported here, so the two interface
classes should not be compared on absolute latency. All clients ran in one
region close to the service endpoint; network round trips are included in
all figures.

\subsection{Experimental scale and cost}

Table~\ref{tab:accounting} reports the exact call accounting of the complete
campaign in two columns that separate the two natural counting
conventions: \emph{successful calls} counts every logged successful
API response, and \emph{unique evaluations} counts distinct
(system, condition, sentence) triples after later re-runs have superseded
earlier responses. In total 43{,}046 successful API responses were
logged: 26{,}238 in the original measurement, 5{,}581 in the extension
sweep, 6{,}675 in the real-speech validation (4{,}650 for the core
six-condition design and 2{,}025 for extending the real-babble ladder from
three to six SNR levels), 3{,}360 for the measured-impulse-response bridge
condition on the synthetic corpus, and 1{,}192 for the second-vendor tasks
(630 GLM reference translations and 562 GLM backend re-translations).
Alongside these, 600 Whisper transcriptions were computed locally without
API access, and 3{,}874 failed attempts were logged, of which 3{,}333 are
LiveTranslate websocket sessions that dropped without recovery and 541 are
API-level failures (throttling, timeouts, protocol errors) that were
retried where the quota allowed. The 875-call difference
between the two columns in the original phase consists of re-runs of the
clipping, packet-loss, and tempo conditions whose later logs superseded the
earlier ones in analysis. Twenty-four logged responses are protocol-level
no-words refusals of the Qwen-Audio system under 0~dB babble; they are
counted as successful calls, scored as empty transcripts, and tabulated
separately in Supplementary Table~S4. The 1{,}544 unique successful
LiveTranslate evaluations of the main grid cover 89.5 percent of that
system's 1{,}728 targets, and the extension added 251 of 432 targeted
sessions; all 450 targeted LiveTranslate sessions
of the real-speech validation completed successfully; the missing sessions
are websocket drops that did not recover within the
retry budget, and every reported LiveTranslate figure states its $n$. The
omni system exhausted its complimentary quota before completing its grid:
its recognition stage covers the main grid on the 144-sentence sweep subset
(119 of 144 sentences at the tempo condition) and its end-to-end
translation path covers 83 to 84 of the 144 sweep sentences per condition,
which is why omni-based comparisons are restricted accordingly and flagged
wherever they appear. The total wall-clock duration of the original data
collection was approximately four hours with up to five systems queried in
parallel. These figures are reported so that other laboratories can
estimate the feasibility of replicating or extending the measurement; they
also demonstrate that a multi-system, multi-condition robustness
characterisation of commercial speech services is attainable at zero
monetary cost with careful quota budgeting, which lowers the barrier for
the periodic re-measurement that the field currently lacks.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

All aggregate error rates and quality scores are reported with 95 percent
bootstrap confidence intervals over sentences, using 1000 resamples. Error
rates are sentence-level: the character error rate for Chinese targets and
the word error rate for English targets, each computed with the standard
Levenshtein alignment, so a Chinese character rate and an English word rate
are pooled only as a macro-average of the two language halves, and an
insertion-dominated transcript can push a rate above 100 percent, which
occurs in the sub-zero-dB regime documented in
Section~\ref{sec:results:snr}. Differences between systems within a
condition are tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}
on paired per-sentence errors, with Holm correction~\cite{holm1979simple}
within the family of all pairwise comparisons of the same condition and
language; because both systems are often simultaneously perfect, the
fraction of zero differences is reported alongside (up to 83 percent under
reverberation), and the test is then driven by the minority of sentences
where the systems differ. Noise severity is characterised three ways: by
the piecewise-linear interpolation of the eight-level curve at the ten
percent error level, which is model-free and is the quantity tabulated in
Table~\ref{tab:slopes}, together with a sentence-level bootstrap
confidence interval of the crossing; by a four-parameter logistic fit to
the same curve, from which the maximum fitted slope in percentage points
per dB measures knee sharpness, with the caveat that the fitted knee
position of the audio-LLM systems falls between 0 and $-5$~dB where no
observations exist, so the fitted maximum is an interpolation between
those levels; and by observed segment slopes between adjacent measured
levels, which involve no model at all. Ordinary least squares slopes over
sub-ranges are reported only for continuity with common practice, and the
text notes how strongly they depend on the chosen range. The relationship
between recognition error and translation quality is summarised by a
linear pass-through regression with explicit intercept, fitted over up to
fifteen operating conditions per cascade. Its uncertainty is reported at
two levels deliberately: sentence-level bootstrap intervals, which reflect
within-condition sampling noise, and condition-level bootstrap intervals
and leave-one-condition-out refits, which reflect how strongly the fitted
slope depends on the particular mix of operating points and are the
appropriate currency for extrapolating the rule to unseen conditions; the
condition-level intervals are substantially wider, and both are reported
wherever the rule is used. Signal processing and statistics use
SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}, librosa~\cite{mcfee2015librosa}, and
pystoi for the short-time objective intelligibility analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:results:snr}, in both the standard and extended (ESTOI)
variants; the tempo condition is excluded from cross-family STOI
comparisons because time-scale modification violates the temporal
alignment that the STOI family presupposes.
